# Chapter 5: The Third Letter

The gale came Saturday morning, three days after I had asked Kova about passage to the mainland and then did not go. The barometer had been falling since Friday noon, steady as a man walking toward a drop he cannot see but knows is there. I wrote the numbers in the log at six-hour intervals — 1018, 1014, 1009 — and drew no rule under any of them because the weather was not yet finished telling me what it meant to say.

By dawn the wind was out of the northeast at twenty knots, gusting higher, and the sea had stopped lying. I went up the ninety-six steps at first light to check the lamp cradle and found everything as it should be: the wick trimmed to regulation width, the glass panels clean, the clockwork wound. I came back down to make coffee and write the morning entry. NNE 22 knots gusting 30. Vis moderate. Pressure 1006 and falling.

The rain started at eight and by nine the visibility had gone to poor and the wind to thirty-five steady. I lit the lamp though it was full day, because that is what the manual says and what my father did and what a gale requires: light in the grey, a mark for the boats that might be stupid enough or desperate enough to run for harbor through this. None came. The island fishermen know the weather the way a man knows his own limp. They had seen the barometer too.

At eleven I went up to check the lamp and wind the clockwork, though it did not need winding yet, because a gale is a thing you tend the way you tend a sick child — past the point of need and into the realm of procedure, which is the only place where fear can be made to sit still. The wind was forty knots and the rain was coming sideways, and the sound of it against the lamp room glass was like gravel thrown by someone with good aim and bad intent.

I came back down to the kitchen and found the letter.

It was wedged under the same stone that had held the second letter, except this time the stone was inside, on the kitchen table, holding the envelope flat against the wood. The door was latched. The windows shut. The wind was forty knots and the rain was the kind that made landing a boat not just stupid but suicidal, and someone had been inside my house and left a letter weighted with a stone while I was ninety-six steps up in the lamp room watching nothing come through the grey.

I did not pick it up right away. I stood in the kitchen and counted my heartbeat against the intervals of the light: four seconds dark, two seconds lit, four seconds dark. Procedure against panic. At the tenth interval I picked up the envelope.

The same paper. The same hand. Dated March 21st — today — and beneath the date, in Danilo's cramped script, a single line that made my hands go cold again, which I had thought they could not do twice for the same cause.

*You are reading this with wet hair.*

I put my hand to my head. My hair was soaked from the walk back down through the rain, plastered to my skull, dripping onto my shoulders. I had not thought about it. I had not thought about anything except the lamp and the log and the fact that the visibility was now poor-to-nil and the wind was still rising.

Two things now true at once: someone was watching me close enough to know when I came down from the lamp room, and no one could have landed a boat in this weather without me seeing it from above or hearing it break apart on the rocks below.

I opened the letter at the kitchen table, with the stone still sitting there patient as a witness, and read what my dead brother had written about the gale that was trying to tear the lighthouse off its rock while I stood inside it.

*The wind is out of the northeast and you have lit the lamp early, which means the visibility has gone and you are following procedure instead of sense. Good. The sea rewards cowards. I underlined the word rewards because you taught it to me and I want you to remember that I remember, even though I was nine and you were lecturing and I was not listening except I was.*

*You are in the kitchen now. You came down wet and did not stop to dry off because a towel is not procedure and a gale is. You are reading this and thinking I could not have known any of these things, but Vera, I know you the way I know when a swell is going to break wrong. I have watched you walk these steps ten thousand times. You are the most predictable thing the sea ever made.*

*Ask me about the money. Ask me what I was going to do with four thousand in cash and why I drew it two days before the storm and where it went when the Morena went down. Ask me why I told Kova I needed to move something heavy and asked him not to mark it in his manifest.*

*You have already decided I meant to run. You are wrong, but you are wrong in a way that is close to right, which is the most dangerous kind of wrong there is.*

*The lamp is still burning. I can see it from where I am, wherever that is. Or I wrote this before, and timed it, and someone is doing what I asked, which is to make you think I can see you. Either way, the effect is the same. You do not know which brother you are reading: the live one or the dead one pretending.*

*The sea rewards cowards. I underlined the word rewards.*

I set the letter down and went to the window. The visibility was nil. The rain was a curtain and the wind was a fist and the sea was the color of wet slate, and there was no boat, and there had been no boat, and no man could have walked up these steps in this weather without my seeing him from the lamp room the way you see a beetle crossing a plate.

I looked at the stone. It was the same stone from the boathouse — I was sure of it now. Not the same kind of stone but the same one, because it had a vein of white quartz through the middle like a scar, and I had marked it without meaning to the first time, the way you mark a thing that does not belong. Someone had carried it from the boathouse to the kitchen, or someone had been carrying it all along, stone to stone and letter to letter, using the weight of the island itself to hold down what my brother had written.

I went back up the ninety-six steps at noon to wind the clockwork and trim the wick. This is procedure: the wick trimmed every six hours to keep the burn even, to keep the light the same height and the same brightness, so that the boats know what they are seeing and the keepers know what they have done. I keep a logbook of it, separate from the weather log — the intervals of trimming written down in a cramped hand that looks, I realized now, too much like my brother's for comfort.

I did not trim the wick.

I wound the clockwork. I checked the glass. I wrote the entry in the lamp log: 1200 hours. Wick service. Clockwork wound. And then I drew a rule under it, as though the task were finished, and I came back down the ninety-six steps into the kitchen and sat at the table with the letter and the stone and waited to see who would notice that the lighthouse keeper had left the wick a finger's width too long.

Outside the wind was forty-five knots and the sea was eating the rocks and the light burned too bright in the grey, the way a lamp burns when the wick is overfed and hungry and not trimmed back to its right and narrow place.

I thought about Danilo at nine, listening to me recite the coast guard manual about cowards and the sea. I thought about the money. I thought about the letter that knew I would come down wet and read it in the kitchen, which was either prophecy or close observation or a brother who had written twenty versions of this letter, one for each place I might have read it, and left them all for someone to deliver on schedule.

The sea rewards cowards, he had written. And then, in case I did not understand: *I underlined the word rewards.*

I looked at the word on the page. The line beneath it was heavy, carved into the paper the way you carve when you press too hard or mean something more than the word itself can carry. Rewards. Not spares. Not forgives. Rewards.

The lamp burned too bright through the afternoon while the gale tore at the glass and I sat in the kitchen and thought about what a man means when he underlines a word his sister taught him twenty years ago, and whether the dead can see the living, and whether it mattered which answer was true.
