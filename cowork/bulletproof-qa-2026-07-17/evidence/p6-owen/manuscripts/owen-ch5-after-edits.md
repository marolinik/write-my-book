The skiff came ashore Tuesday on the north shingle, stern first, the way boats arrive when nobody is steering them. I saw that the rope had frayed where it crossed the iron ring. The rest of her was sound: hull tight, thwarts dry, a small boat with a small cabin forward, though calling it a cabin flatters both the boat and the word. From the lamp gallery the harbor looked small too, and I wrote none of that in the log, because the log does not take opinions.

What I wrote was: "SW 4 knots. Vis good. One skiff, north shingle, no oars." Then I drew the line.

No oars. None. A boat with no oars arrives the way a bill arrives: someone else has already decided you owe.

I went down after the noon service and looked at her properly. The knots at the bow were tied wet — you could see it in how they had dried, the fibers sprung and pale — which meant whoever tied them was in a hurry or in the dark, and neither of those is how a man ties up a boat he means to come back for.

Two things true at once: the skiff was Kova's, and Kova had told me on Thursday that he had sold her in the spring.

The shingle held its quiet while I stood with my hand on her gunwale and worked out which of those facts I would mention to him first. Behind the island the light failed at 18:40. Barometer steady. What held me was the smaller arithmetic: the rope and the ring and the salt that held them together, and the tin I found wedged under the stone I had found the first letter under, weeks ago, when this was still a story about grief.

Inside the tin: nothing. Which is not the same as empty. A man who leaves an empty tin under a marked stone is saying I was here and took it with me. Walking back up the steps, the lamp seemed dimmer than it should, though the wick was right and the glass was clean.

That night I wrote: "Skiff secured above tide line. Tin found. Contents: gone before me." Then I did not draw the line, because whoever emptied the tin was not finished, and neither was I.
