I went down to the village Thursday for kerosene and came back with intelligence instead. Intelligence is the village's only reliable export. It appears on no manifest.

Radmila's shop sits where the harbor road bends, which means she sees every boat before it lands and every man before he lies about where he has been. The co-op meets in her back room. The fishermen settle their accounts at her table. If you want to know what the island knows, you ask Radmila. If you want to know what the island suspects, you watch who asks her.

She was slicing bread when I came in. She did not look up.

"The kerosene is in back," she said. "And there are eggs. Take them. You look like you have not eaten an egg since Easter."

"I eat."

"Soup is not eating. Soup is what you do when you have forgotten what food is for." She wrapped the loaf in paper that crackled. "The co-op put the deposit through Tuesday. Four thousand. I wanted you to hear it from me before someone said it badly."

I had come down the ninety-six steps to ask about that four thousand. Danilo's second letter had named the money, and named Radmila, and I had spent Wednesday deciding how to ask the question without admitting I already knew to ask it. Now I did not have to. The question had asked itself, which is the village's other export.

"The deposit," I said.

"Against the Morena's loss. The policy was current. Your brother paid it in January, which surprised the whole co-op, because he paid in April most years, with a story about the winter catch." She set the loaf down and looked at me for the first time. "You did not know."

It was not a question, so I did not answer it. The co-op keeps every boat on the island in one ledger; a policy is cheaper bought together than alone. That much I knew. What I had not known was that my brother had ever paid a thing on time, or that he had four thousand to put anywhere but into the sea, which was where the rest of him had gone.

"Who came to tell me?" I asked.

"No one came. The co-op does not send men up to a lighthouse with news about money." She wiped her knife on her apron. "I am telling you because there are two more at the harbor asking about cargo manifests, and they have a lawyer's face and a company name no one here has heard, and I thought you should meet that news standing up."

I picked up the two cans of kerosene and carried them to the counter without asking who the men were. Radmila wrapped the eggs in yesterday's newspaper and set them in the basket beside the bread.

"They are not from the insurance," she said. "Different soft hands."

I paid her and walked down to the harbor.

* * *

The two of them wore jackets that had never met salt and shoes that clicked on the stone. One held a clipboard with a laminated card clipped to the top of it. The other kept his hands in his pockets the way a man does when he is working to look comfortable and cannot manage it. They stood by Kova's boat in its cradle, studying it the way you study a thing while you wait for its owner to arrive.

I set the kerosene down and said, "The Morena's manifest is with the co-op. Radmila keeps the records."

The clipboard turned. "You are the sister."

"I am the keeper. The sister is up at the light."

He smiled, a small smile that meant he had heard the joke and knew I was inside it. "We represent a lender. A private arrangement. Your brother carried an outstanding obligation."

"My brother carried a boat. The boat is on the rocks off Struga. You can see it at low tide if the light is right."

"The boat was collateral," said the one with his hands in his pockets. "The obligation is not on the rocks."

"Then you should raise it with the sea. The sea has the collateral now."

The clipboard made a note. "There were personal effects. Documentation. A manifest would show what cargo the vessel was carrying when she wrecked."

"A manifest would show line and diesel and the usual provisions. I do not know what you imagine my brother was carrying."

"We imagine he was carrying payment," said the soft-handed one, and his voice was softer than his hands, which is how I understood he was not from any bank that keeps its name on a door.

I heard two things at once in that sentence: that they did not have the money, and that they believed I might. Both were useful. Neither was mine to correct.

I lifted the kerosene. "If you dredge four thousand off the bottom, send word. The co-op would like it back."

I walked past them and up the harbor road, and neither followed, because they had already learned the one thing they had come for, which was that I did not know where the money was either. I had told them nothing but the truth, and given them nothing at all. It is a narrow trick and my brother taught me the whole of it, years ago, across a card table, laughing.

That night I wrote in the log: "NNE 9 knots. Vis good. Two men, mainland. Asked the wrong question of the right woman." Then I drew the line under it, the way you close a door you do not want tried in the night.

* * *

Radmila was still behind the counter when I came back through for the eggs I had left. The shop smelled of fennel and old wood and the particular smell of a room where people have argued about small sums of money for several generations.

"They found you," she said.

"They found the sea. I told them where it was."

She reached under the counter and brought up a round of cheese, and cut a wedge from it, and laid it on a square of paper and began folding the corners in, the way you wrap a thing you expect to travel a distance.

"Your brother bought paper from me. Good paper. For years." She pressed the last fold flat with her thumb. "Who writes that many letters?"

I stood in the doorway with the kerosene dragging at my arms and the eggs in the basket and the question hanging between us like a bell that someone else had rung.

"I do not know," I said.

And that, at least, was true.
